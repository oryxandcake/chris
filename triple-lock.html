Part 1

Where the UK’s £1.29 trillion goes

In 2024–25 the UK government spent £1,288 billion — about 44.0% of GDP. To see whether the State Pension is on track to “consume the economy,” it helps to first see how big it is next to everything else the state pays for.

The chart below breaks public-service spending into its official functions. Social protection — pensions and benefits combined — is the single biggest area. The State Pension alone (the orange block) is roughly 4.7% of GDP: bigger than defence, schools or debt interest individually, but still a long way short of swallowing the whole pie.

Part 2

How the triple lock works — and why it “ratchets”

Since 2011 the State Pension has risen every April by the highest of three measures:

1. average earnings growth  •  2. CPI inflation  •  3. a flat 2.5% floor

Because the pension always takes the best of the three, it never has a “bad year” relative to wages. In a year of high inflation it tracks prices; in a flat year it still gets 2.5%. Recent rises show the pattern: +10.1% in 2023 (inflation), +8.5% in 2024 (earnings), +4.1% in 2025 (earnings).

This is the ratchet effect. Over a full economic cycle the pension gains a little more than earnings — and since GDP per person roughly tracks earnings, the pension’s share of the economy drifts upward and never settles back. The OBR notes the non-earnings parts of the lock were triggered in 8 of the 13 years to 2025. This “no equilibrium” property is the seed of truth inside the 100%-of-GDP claim — and we test exactly where it leads in Part 4.

Part 3

What the OBR actually projects

The Office for Budget Responsibility — the government’s independent fiscal watchdog — publishes the official long-term projections. Its July 2025 Fiscal Risks and Sustainability report is the authoritative source here. It does not forecast pensions reaching 100% of GDP.

Instead it projects State Pension spending rising from about 5% of GDP today to 7.7% by the early 2070s. Of that 2.7-point rise, the OBR attributes 1.6 points to the triple lock itself (the rest is the ageing population). Health spending is the bigger pressure — projected to climb from ~8% to nearly 15% of GDP.

The OBR’s real warning is not about the 2070s pension line — it is about the total. With health, pensions and an ageing population combined, and on unchanged policy, it projects public debt rising above 270% of GDP by the early 2070s, calling the path “unsustainable.” That is the genuine, dated problem — far sooner and far more concrete than “100% of GDP.”

Part 4

So — 100% of GDP?

Here is the logic behind the claim. Because the triple lock has no stable share of GDP (Part 2), if you assume it runs completely unchanged forever, its share doesn’t level off — it keeps compounding. Compound growth with no ceiling will, given enough time, cross any line you draw, including 100%.

So the question isn’t really whether — it is how long. The chart below extrapolates the ratchet forward. Drag the slider to set how fast the pension outpaces GDP each year, and watch when the line reaches 100%.

The result is the honest answer to the claim. Even on the OBR’s volatile-economy assumption — a deliberately pessimistic case — the pension does not reach 100% of GDP until around the year 2300s. On the OBR’s central assumption it is the 2500s. On the Treasury’s original 2012 estimate it is past the year 3500. Long before any of that, the curve would pass the size of the entire current UK state (44% of GDP) — and long before that, the policy would have been changed.

Verdict

Misleading — but built on a real flaw

What the claim gets right

The triple lock genuinely has no stable share of GDP. By guaranteeing the best of earnings, inflation or 2.5%, it tends to outgrow the economy. Run unchanged forever, its share rises without limit — so “eventually 100%” is a real mathematical property, not nonsense.

Why it is misleading

“Eventually” means roughly 300 to 1,500 years, depending on assumptions — and it is not a forecast anyone makes. The OBR’s actual central projection is 7.7% of GDP by the early 2070s, up from ~5%. No policy in history has survived unchanged for three centuries.

The real problem — this century

The triple lock is set to cost £15.5bn a year more than earnings-uprating by 2029–30 (three times the original estimate). Combined with health and ageing, the OBR projects national debt above 270% of GDP by the 2070s on unchanged policy. That — not the year 2300 — is why the OBR calls the triple lock a “significant” fiscal risk.

Methodology & assumptions

The extrapolation in Part 4 models State Pension spending as a share of GDP, P, starting at 5.0% in 2025 (the OBR’s rounded current figure) and compounding:

  • P(t) = 5.0% × (1 + r)t, where t is years after 2025 and r is the “ratchet rate” — the average amount by which the pension outpaces GDP each year.
  • It reaches 100% of GDP when t = ln(20) / ln(1 + r).
  • r = 0.6% reproduces the OBR’s stated result: it lifts the pension to ~6.7% of GDP by 2073, matching the 1.6-percentage-point triple-lock contribution in the July 2025 report.
  • r = 1.0% reproduces the OBR’s “volatile economy” case — the 1.6-point contribution plus the extra 1.5% of GDP the OBR says persistent inflation/earnings volatility could add by the 2070s.
  • r = 0.2% is the assumption used when the triple lock was introduced in 2012 (pension growth ~0.2 points above earnings).

This is a deliberately simple long-run model. It isolates the triple-lock ratchet and treats the pensioner share of the population as broadly stable over the very long run (it is bounded — it cannot rise forever), so the ratchet is the only unbounded driver. It ignores future policy change entirely, which is precisely why it is an illustration of the mechanism, not a forecast. The OBR’s own projections (Part 3) are the proper forecasts and stop in the 2070s.
